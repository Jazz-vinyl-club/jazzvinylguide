# Red Clay on Vinyl: The Definitive Pressing Guide

## Summary

*Red Clay* was recorded over three days in late January 1970 – the 27th, 28th, and 29th – at Rudy Van Gelder's studio in Englewood Cliffs, NJ, produced by Creed Taylor and engineered by Van Gelder himself. It was Freddie Hubbard's first album for Taylor's newly independent CTI label, following a seven-year run on Blue Note and a subsequent stretch on Atlantic.

This is a landmark early-fusion recording from Van Gelder's studio, mixing electric and acoustic instrumentation in a way that rewards a clean, well-separated pressing – muddy copies tend to smear Hancock's electric piano into the rhythm section rather than let it sit distinctly alongside Carter's bass and White's kick drum.

**Best early pressing for audiophiles:** [CTI 6001](https://www.discogs.com/release/1583965) – 1970 original pressing, Terre Haute plant, orange Epic-era CTI labels.

**Best modern pressing for audiophiles:** [ORG Music ORGM-2000](https://www.discogs.com/release/13543935) (2014) – mastered by Bernie Grundman from the original analog tapes, pressed at Pallas in Germany, 45rpm.

**Best-value modern:** [ORG Music ORGM-1085](https://www.discogs.com/release/9987034) – single-LP 33⅓ reissue, also mastered AAA by Bernie Grundman.

## Recording history and tape provenance

The quintet Taylor assembled was a decisive statement in itself: Hubbard on trumpet, Joe Henderson on tenor sax and flute, Herbie Hancock on Fender Rhodes and organ, Ron Carter on electric and acoustic bass, and Lenny White on drums – Hancock and Carter fresh off their famous run in Miles Davis's second great quintet, Henderson established as a Blue Note leader in his own right, and White, by far the youngest and least established name in the room, providing what many critics consider the record's real catalyst. Hubbard reportedly wanted the full Davis rhythm section, including Tony Williams on drums, but Williams turned the gig down for reasons that were never entirely clear; White got the call instead, on the strength of his recent work on Miles Davis's *Bitches Brew* sessions. White brought a homemade 16-inch bass drum built from an old oil drum to the date, but the setup caused a resonance problem with Carter's bass that forced Van Gelder to swap in a 28-inch bass drum mid-session – nearly double the diameter White was used to playing. Creed Taylor liked the resulting sound; White reportedly disliked it for years afterward, and the unusually prominent, slightly ungainly kick drum audible on the title track is a direct result of that last-minute swap, not a mixing or mastering artifact on any particular pressing.

The title track has since become a jazz standard in its own right and one of the most frequently sampled sources in golden-era hip-hop, most famously by A Tribe Called Quest. One packaging quirk worth knowing before you go shopping: original CTI 6001 gatefolds shipped with at least three different interior cover-art color treatments – a red-clay-colored photo, a gold-toned version, and a white version with different photography entirely – all of them legitimate CTI 6001 pressings, not a printing error unique to any single copy. Note also: some streaming-service descriptions of this album list Stanley Turrentine as a performer, a credit that doesn't appear in Wikipedia, the original LP credits, or any collector discography consulted for this guide, and looks like a mix-up with a different CTI-era session – it's been left out of this guide rather than repeated as fact.

Collectors report real, audible quality variation even among pressings sharing the same catalog number, more so than is typical for a title this well-regarded.

## Sound quality by pressing

The true first pressing, CTI 6001, Terre Haute pressing (1970 original), is on CTI's original glossy gatefold packaging with cover photography by Price Givens and design by Tony Lane. This catalog number saw many pressing-plant and label-color variants over the years – Terre Haute, Santa Maria, and Carrollton, GA domestic pressings, plus various European pressings on orange, blue, and grey Epic labels – and collector reports describe real quality differences between plants for this specific title. Confirm the Terre Haute plant and orange labels specifically if you're chasing a genuine, well-regarded first pressing.

The ORG Music ORGM-2000 (2014, 45rpm) is the reference pressing. Splitting the album across four 45rpm sides widens the groove spacing, and owner reviews describe it as a top-tier pressing overall, with one recurring note of mild background hiss on "Delphia" that appears to be a tape-source characteristic rather than a pressing defect specific to this reissue.

The ORG Music ORGM-1085 (33⅓ RPM) is a single-LP, standard-speed ORG reissue, also mastered AAA by Bernie Grundman from the original tapes. Owner reviews describe it as flat, centered, and dynamic, with Van Gelder's original room sound coming through clearly – a good option for collectors who want Grundman's mastering without the 45rpm reissue's higher price and extra disc-handling. Also repackaged in 2017 as a Barnes & Noble-exclusive red vinyl edition with identical content and mastering, confirmed directly on the Discogs listing for that specific pressing.

## Buyer's guide by budget

**$$:** The [ORG Music ORGM-1085](https://www.discogs.com/release/9987034) 33⅓ reissue, including its 2017 Barnes & Noble red vinyl repackaging.

**$$$:** A clean [CTI 6001](https://www.discogs.com/release/1583965) original – confirm the Terre Haute plant and orange labels for a genuine early pressing; at least one experienced collector reports a UK pressing sounding notably crisper, though the specific UK label/plant details weren't confirmed here.

**$$$$:** The [ORG Music ORGM-2000](https://www.discogs.com/release/13543935) 45rpm reissue.
