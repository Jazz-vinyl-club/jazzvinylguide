# The Atomic Mr. Basie on Vinyl: The Definitive Pressing Guide

## A note on the title before anything else

This album's own original packaging doesn't agree with itself on what it's called. The front cover reads simply "Basie" (styled as "E=MC²"), while the rear cover, spine, and record labels all read "The Atomic Mr. Basie." The nickname stuck because of the cover's striking mushroom-cloud photograph, and "The Atomic Mr. Basie" is now the name used almost universally, including by this guide – but if you're searching listings, don't be thrown by a seller describing an obviously identical record as simply "Basie" or "E=MC²." All three refer to the same album.

## Summary

The album was recorded October 21–22, 1957 at Capitol Studios in New York City, produced by Teddy Reig, and was Count Basie's first release for the newly formed Roulette label after leaving Verve. Arranger Neal Hefti wrote all eleven tracks on the original release, giving Basie's orchestra a leaner, more modern-sounding book than his 1930s-40s material while still built for hard swing – tenor saxophonist Eddie "Lockjaw" Davis gets substantial solo space throughout, and the trumpet section (led by Thad Jones, with Joe Newman, Wendell Culley, and Snooky Young) carries much of the record's explosive character. The album won two awards at the 1st Annual Grammy Awards in 1959: Best Jazz Performance, Group, and Best Performance by a Dance Band. AllMusic's Bruce Eder called it a record that "took Basie's core audience and a lot of other people by surprise, as a bold, forward-looking statement," and it appears in the book *1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die*, with Will Fulford-Jones describing it as "Basie's last great record."

Part of the second day of the October 1957 sessions was actually used to record two additional tracks arranged by Jimmy Mundy ("Silks and Satins" and "Sleepwalker's Serenade") along with a version of "The Late, Late Show" – none of which appeared on the original 1958 LP. Those tracks surfaced only in 1994, on an expanded CD reissue titled *The Complete Atomic Basie*, which runs 56:34 versus the original LP's 39:30 and, per collector reports, uses a different overall track sequence rather than simply appending the extra material at the end. If you're specifically after the original eleven-track program in its original sequence, make sure whatever you're buying isn't a repackaging of the expanded Complete Atomic Basie edit.

**Best early pressing for audiophiles:** an original US Roulette R-52003 mono pressing – see the note below on this title's unusually sprawling reissue landscape before assuming any specific reissue is a safe upgrade.

**Best modern pressing for audiophiles:** [Classic Records' 200-gram QUIEX SV-P reissue](https://www.discogs.com/release/3080100) – matrix-confirmed mastering by Bernie Grundman.

**Best-value modern:** treat any other specific reissue claim with real caution given the discography complexity described below; a later, clearly-labeled repress of the original eleven-track program is the safer default over an unfamiliar colored-vinyl import.

## A genuine audiophile reissue found on closer review, and a sprawling landscape beyond it

An earlier pass on this guide stated that no dedicated audiophile treatment of this title could be confirmed. That was wrong, and worth correcting plainly: Classic Records issued a 200-gram QUIEX SV-P Super Vinyl pressing of this title, and the matrix runout on that specific release is etched "R-52003-A BG" / "R-52003 B BG" – "BG" being Bernie Grundman's standard runout signature, confirming his mastering directly from the physical evidence rather than promotional copy. This is a real, verifiable audiophile pressing that should have been the headline recommendation from the start. One piece of context worth knowing before assuming it's an automatic upgrade over an original: a dealer site (The Skeptical Audiophile) that has reviewed a large number of Bernie Grundman's Classic Records-era work expresses broad skepticism toward that catalog generally, describing several Classic titles as never sounding better than a genuine original pressing – I did not find a review from that source specifically evaluating this Basie title's Classic pressing, so this is general context about the era and engineer rather than a confirmed verdict on this specific record.

Beyond that confirmed reissue, Discogs' master listing for this title still documents 144 separate versions – an extraordinary number even by the standards of a well-loved 1958 record, reflecting decades of licensing across multiple countries and labels (UK pressings via EMI/Columbia under license from Roulette, budget-label European pressings like a Swedish Sonet "Grand Prix" edition, Spanish colored-vinyl imports, DMM European reissues, and more). One documented tracklist discrepancy worth flagging: at least one UK-pressed copy is missing two tracks present on the original US program ("Double-O" and "Midnite Blue"), per a Discogs commenter who grew up with that specific pressing and only later realized it was incomplete relative to the full US release. Given that kind of variance, this guide is still not presenting a fully confident price-tier table for every pressing beyond the two confirmed above – verify track count and country of origin carefully before buying anything outside the two entries this guide can vouch for directly.

## Buyer's guide by budget

Given the sourcing gaps described above beyond the two confirmed pressings, this guide isn't presenting confident dollar-sign price tiers across the board for this title. A clean [Classic Records 200-gram QUIEX SV-P pressing](https://www.discogs.com/release/3080100) is the confirmed audiophile choice, with Bernie Grundman's mastering verified via the runout etching itself. A clearly labeled original US Roulette R-52003 pressing, condition permitting, is the vintage-character alternative. Beyond those two, verify the specific pressing, country of origin, and full track count of anything you're considering – given 144 documented versions and at least one confirmed case of a UK pressing missing two tracks from the original US program, assuming any two copies sharing a cover image are equivalent is a real risk with this particular title.
