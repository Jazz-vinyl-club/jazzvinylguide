# Piano Starts Here on Vinyl: The Definitive Pressing Guide

## A note on what this album actually is

This is not a studio album in the usual sense – it's a compilation, assembled by Columbia in 1968, of two completely unrelated Art Tatum recordings made sixteen years apart. Tracks 1 through 4 are Tatum's first-ever commercial recordings, made for Brunswick Records in March 1933 in New York City. Every other track comes from a live performance at a Gene Norman-produced "Just Jazz" concert at the Shrine Auditorium in Los Angeles in May 1949. Columbia paired these two sources onto a single LP for the first time in 1968, titled *Piano Starts Here* – a reference to Tatum's beginnings, since the 1933 sides really were where his recorded career started. If you're expecting a coherent studio session with consistent recording quality throughout, this isn't that record; it's a historical anthology bridging the very start and a mid-career high point of Tatum's recording life.

## Summary

Tatum was, by wide critical consensus, one of the most technically extraordinary pianists jazz has ever produced – a stride-rooted virtuoso whose harmonic reharmonizations and sheer speed reportedly left even other elite pianists stunned on first hearing him. The 1933 Brunswick sides capture him at 23, already fully formed technically. The 1949 concert material, recorded live at the Shrine Auditorium, captures him sixteen years later in a looser, audience-facing setting. One archival note worth passing along with appropriate hedging: an Internet Archive uploader speculates that the closing track – originally a medley of "The Man I Love" into selections from *Porgy and Bess* with a reprise of "The Man I Love" – may have been edited on this release, possibly to reduce royalty costs, though this is a stated guess rather than a confirmed fact.

A genuinely important technical caveat applies to the original 1968 release: it's an "electronically simulated" stereo pressing of source recordings that were originally mono, both the 1933 studio sides and the 1949 live concert. Electronically reprocessed fake stereo, created by artificially splitting a mono signal across two channels, is generally considered a sonic compromise rather than an enhancement by collectors and audio engineers – it doesn't add real channel-separated information that wasn't in the original mono recording, and can introduce phasing artifacts. If a genuine mono pressing of this material exists, it would likely be preferable to the "two-eyed" electronically-simulated stereo version for listeners who care about hearing the recordings as close to their original form as possible – though this guide was not able to independently confirm whether Columbia issued a true mono pressing of this specific compilation alongside the stereo one.

**Best early pressing for audiophiles:** an original 1968 Columbia CS 9655 pressing – but see the note above on its electronically-simulated stereo before assuming "original" automatically means "best-sounding."

**Best modern pressing for audiophiles:** not confirmed in this pass – no dedicated audiophile reissue of this specific compilation was found during research.

**Best-value modern:** later Columbia/CBS pressings under the same or a similar catalog number – condition and specific pressing details matter more than any particular year here, given the fake-stereo caveat applies broadly across this compilation's vinyl history.

## Buyer's guide by budget

Given the sourcing gaps and the fake-stereo caveat above, this guide isn't presenting confident dollar-sign price tiers for this title. Any copy of Columbia CS 9655, from the 1968 original through later repressings, likely carries the same electronically-simulated stereo characteristic described above – there's no clearly documented "better" reissue to steer toward instead. If owning this specific historical pairing of Tatum's earliest and a strong mid-career live set matters to you, condition and price are the main variables to weigh, rather than chasing a specific pressing year.
