# The Hot Five and Hot Seven Sessions on Vinyl: The Definitive Pressing Guide

## A note on what this "album" actually is

There is no original album here in the sense every other guide on this site describes. Louis Armstrong's Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings are 89 individual sides, cut for OKeh Records (with a few related sessions for Vocalion, Brunswick, and Columbia) between November 1925 and roughly 1930, each originally issued as a standalone 78rpm single years before the 12" LP format existed at all. The idea of these recordings as a unified "album" is entirely a later, retrospective packaging decision by reissue labels – meaning, unlike every other guide here, there's no original pressing to chase, and no single canonical modern edition either. This guide focuses on the vintage vinyl-era compilations that actually exist, and flags a real gap: the two most respected modern "complete" scholarly reissues of this material were never released on vinyl at all, only CD.

## Summary

Recorded in Chicago (with the final sessions in New York), the Hot Five – Armstrong on cornet and trumpet, his wife Lil Hardin Armstrong on piano, Kid Ory on trombone, Johnny Dodds on clarinet, and Johnny St. Cyr on banjo – began recording for OKeh in November 1925. By May 1927 the group expanded with the addition of tuba player Pete Briggs and drummer Baby Dodds, becoming the Hot Seven for a concentrated run of sessions recorded across just eight days that month. Among the 89 total sides are recordings now considered foundational to jazz as an art form built around individual improvisation rather than collective New Orleans-style ensemble playing – "Potato Head Blues," "Wild Man Blues," "Hotter Than That," "Cornet Chop Suey," and "West End Blues" among them. "Melancholy Blues," from the Hot Seven sessions, was selected for inclusion on the Voyager Golden Record, launched into space in 1977 as part of humanity's cultural message to any extraterrestrial life that might find it.

**Best early pressing for audiophiles:** an original OKeh or Columbia 78rpm single of any individual recording – the true historical artifact, though acquiring a complete run this way is a serious, expensive undertaking given how many individual sides exist across multiple original labels.

**Best modern pressing for audiophiles:** not confirmed – see below on a real gap between the best-regarded complete reissues and what's actually available on vinyl.

**Best-value modern:** a vintage-era Columbia "Louis Armstrong Story" 12" LP (1950s), covering a specific subset of sessions per volume – see below on how this series divides the material.

## Recording history and tape provenance

Columbia's "Louis Armstrong Story" series, issued on 12" LP in the 1950s (with reissues into the 1960s on the label's six-eye design), was the first attempt to bring this material to the long-playing format domestically, organized by session rather than as one complete anthology – Volume II, for instance, consists entirely of the May 1927 Hot Seven sessions originally cut for OKeh. Because the series divides the total body of recordings across multiple separate volumes rather than presenting them as a single unified "album," collecting the complete Hot Five/Hot Seven output through this route means tracking down several individual LPs rather than one.

Modern scholarship has produced two widely respected "complete" compilations, and it's worth knowing neither is available on vinyl. Columbia/Legacy's 2000 box set, *The Complete Hot Five and Hot Seven Recordings*, used the original masters for its transfers and expanded the scope to include related sessions beyond the core Hot Five/Seven billing – Lil Armstrong's "Lil's Hot Shots," sessions with Johnny Dodds, and other contemporaneous small-group dates – presented across four CDs in a hardback book. AllMusic called it "beyond indispensable." The same year, JSP Records issued a competing four-disc set, *Hot Fives & Sevens*, remastered by John R. T. Davies and later reissued on the Definitive label; the Penguin Guide to Jazz gave it a "crown" as a core-collection pick, and later critics have genuinely disagreed about which of the two "complete" sets sounds better – Ben Ratliff preferred the Columbia edition, while Penguin's own editors called the JSP remastering "superlative." Neither of these two most-respected complete reissues, as far as this research could confirm, has ever been issued on vinyl; both exist only as CD box sets. For a collector who specifically wants both the vinyl format and the fullest, most scholarly-regarded version of this material, that's a real and unresolved gap rather than a simple "buy the best reissue" recommendation.

Vinyl-era compilations from outside the US do exist and offer partial coverage. A German MCA Records "Club Edition" release (catalog H 283/4) and a separate German Electrola pressing (*Armstrong Hot Five and Seven, Vol. 1*) both compile selections from these sessions on vinyl, though neither claims or achieves the completeness of the modern CD box sets. Individual original recordings also circulated on UK Parlophone 78rpm singles under license from OKeh – "Alligator Blues," for instance, was issued on Parlophone catalog R 2185 – a reminder that for collectors specifically chasing original-era artifacts rather than any later compilation, individual UK or European 78rpm pressings of specific tracks are a real, if piecemeal, alternative to US originals.

## Buyer's guide by budget

Given that no single modern, complete, vinyl edition of this material exists, this guide isn't presenting confident dollar-sign price tiers or a "best overall" recommendation the way most guides here do. If you want vintage-era US vinyl specifically, hunting down individual volumes of Columbia's "Louis Armstrong Story" series is the most direct route, understanding you'll need multiple LPs to cover the full scope of sessions. If you're open to European vinyl compilations, the German MCA and Electrola pressings offer partial coverage in a single package. If completeness matters more than format, the CD box sets (Columbia/Legacy or JSP) remain the most respected options, with no vinyl equivalent currently available.
