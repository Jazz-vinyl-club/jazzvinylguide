# Free Jazz on Vinyl: The Definitive Pressing Guide

## Summary

*Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation* was recorded December 21, 1960 at A&R Studios in New York City, produced by Nesuhi Ertegun. Ornette Coleman's concept was audacious even by the standards of an artist already known for upending convention: two complete jazz quartets playing simultaneously in the same room, improvising together for over 36 minutes with no predetermined structure, no splicing, and no overdubs. Released September 1961 as Atlantic 1364 (mono) and SD-1364 (stereo).

**Best early pressing for audiophiles:** [Atlantic 1364 mono](https://www.discogs.com/release/1942501) or [SD-1364 stereo](https://www.discogs.com/release/370754) – 1961 original pressings.

**Best modern pressing for audiophiles:** the ORG Music 45rpm 2LP edition – mastered by Bernie Grundman from the original analog tapes, pressed at Pallas in Germany.

**Best-value modern:** Speakers Corner's 180g reissue – see below on a documented quality-control issue before buying.

## Recording history and tape provenance

Coleman's regular working quartet – himself on alto sax, Don Cherry on pocket trumpet, Scott LaFaro on bass, and Billy Higgins on drums – occupies the left channel; a second quartet featuring Eric Dolphy on bass clarinet, Freddie Hubbard on trumpet, Charlie Haden on bass, and Ed Blackwell on drums occupies the right. The album's title effectively named an entire subsequent genre, even though the term "free jazz" had existed earlier with an unrelated meaning (jazz performed without an admission charge).

The record's reception was genuinely split at the time, memorably captured by DownBeat magazine's decision to run a double review of the same album: Pete Welding awarded it the maximum five stars, while John Tynan – reportedly the most conservative of the era's jazz critics – gave it zero. The cover reproduces Jackson Pollock's painting "White Light," a deliberate visual analogy for the music inside – abstract, dense, and resistant to a single fixed reading. Decades later, the album's cover concept would itself become a reference point, inspiring the artwork for Floating Points, Pharoah Sanders, and the London Symphony Orchestra's 2021 album *Promises*.

This is a case where flagging a documented pressing problem matters more than usual, since it affects a currently in-print, easy-to-find pressing rather than an obscure vintage variant. Speakers Corner's 2021 reissue (catalog AATL 1364, pressed at Pallas in Germany from the original analog master tapes) is well-regarded in principle – all-analog mastering, faithful reproduction of the original artwork and die-cut gatefold. But multiple Discogs owner reviews of this specific pressing independently report "non-fill," a real vinyl manufacturing defect where molten vinyl doesn't completely fill the stamper mold during pressing, causing audible dropouts or missing groove sections rather than just surface noise. Reviews range from "no issues with my press" to "way too much non-fill on this" for the same catalog number, suggesting inconsistent quality control across the pressing run rather than a universal defect – worth inspecting a copy carefully, or buying from a seller with a clear return policy, rather than assuming any given copy is representative.

This isn't unique to Speakers Corner's edition, either – one Discogs comment about the broader reissue history of this title more generally observes that "out of the large number of reissues, only a very small number of pieces of acceptable quality appear," while separately noting that mono original pressings tend to be unusually resilient and play reliably regardless of condition. A separate owner review of the ORG Music 45rpm pressing independently corroborates the Speakers Corner issue from the other direction: that reviewer explicitly states they bought the ORG edition specifically because non-fill on their Speakers Corner copy bothered them enough to seek out an alternative pressing entirely.

## Sound quality by pressing

The Atlantic 1364 (mono, 1961 original) is identifiable by an orange and purple label with a white fan logo on a black background, and a die-cut gatefold jacket. One documented mono pressing carries the matrix "11807-A AT. W J" (side A) and "11808-A AT. W J" (side B). Given the recording's hard-panned two-quartet stereo separation, some listeners specifically prefer the mono fold-down for a more unified listening experience.

The Atlantic SD-1364 (stereo, 1961 original) is identifiable by a blue and green label with a white fan logo on black. This is the pressing that actually delivers Coleman's intended channel separation between the two quartets, which is central to how the album was conceived and mixed.

The ORG Music 45rpm 2LP is mastered by Bernie Grundman from the original analog tapes and pressed at Pallas in Germany. ORG's own promotional description makes a reasonable technical case for why this particular recording benefits unusually well from a 45rpm cut: since the album is presented in two hard-panned stereo channels rather than a blended mix, the wider groove spacing a 45rpm cut allows helps keep the two quartets' channels distinct rather than bleeding into each other.

A well-regarded 1972 Japanese pressing also circulates among collectors, described by one reviewer as sounding "pretty good" – a reasonable option if you're specifically after a vintage-era pressing but an original US copy proves hard to source or is priced high. A specific catalog number for it could not be independently confirmed.

## Buyer's guide by budget

**$$:** A used copy of an earlier reissue (1970s–90s Atlantic pressings exist, sharing the SD-1364 catalog number with later label variants) – a reasonable way in if you're not chasing an original or the current Speakers Corner pressing.

**$$$:** Speakers Corner's 180g reissue – inspect carefully or buy from a seller with a clear return policy given the documented non-fill issue.

**$$$$:** A clean original [Atlantic 1364 mono](https://www.discogs.com/release/1942501) or [SD-1364 stereo](https://www.discogs.com/release/370754) pressing, condition-dependent.

**$$$$$:** The ORG Music 45rpm 2LP edition – the modern reference for hearing the two-quartet channel separation as intended.
